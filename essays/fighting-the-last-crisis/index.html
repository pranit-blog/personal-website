Every System Built to Prevent the Last Crisis Is Optimized to Miss the Next One

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Aug 24, 2026
7 min read
featured image

Silicon Valley Bank followed the rules built after 2008. It failed anyway, because those rules were built for a different bank breaking a different rule fifteen years earlier.

In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank collapsed because it had done almost everything post-2008 regulation asked of it. It held government securities. It avoided the exotic derivatives that sank AIG. It looked, on paper, like exactly the kind of bank regulators wanted after 2008.

It failed anyway. Not because it broke the rules. Because the rules were built to catch a different bank breaking a different rule fifteen years earlier.

This is not a story about SVB's management being careless. It's a story about what happens every time a system gets built after a crisis instead of before one. I spent years inside banking supervision, watching this pattern from the inside. It never stops repeating, and it never stops surprising people who should know better.

We Regulate the Rearview Mirror

Every major regulatory framework in finance was written in response to something that already happened. The 1988 Basel Accord followed the Savings and Loan crisis. Dodd-Frank followed 2008. Each one was a detailed, well-intentioned answer to a question the world had already stopped asking.

The problem is structural, not a failure of any one regulator. Rules get written by looking backward at the last failure and asking how to prevent that specific failure from happening again. But risk doesn't sit still and wait to be regulated. It moves.

After the S&L crisis, tighter capital rules pushed banks toward securitization to hold less regulatory capital while keeping the same risk exposure. That workaround quietly built the shadow banking system. Twenty years later, that same shadow banking system helped blow up the global economy. The 2008 rules were, in effect, an answer to a question created by the 1988 rules.

Then came SVB. Regulators had spent a decade and a half worrying about banks being overexposed through derivatives and complex risk. SVB was the opposite problem: a bank that was under-hedged against interest rate risk, sitting in the plainest, most "safe" asset there is, government bonds. Nobody had written a rule against that, because nobody had needed to. Until they did.

This is the honest version of the finance story. It's not that regulation doesn't work. It's that regulation is very good at preventing yesterday's disaster and structurally blind to tomorrow's, because tomorrow's disaster is, almost by definition, shaped exactly to avoid triggering yesterday's rule.

Detection, Not Prevention

Here's the part regulators rarely say out loud: most of what gets called "prevention" is actually detection dressed up in prevention's clothing.

A rule that says "hold more capital against mortgage-backed securities" doesn't prevent a novel kind of crisis. It detects and blocks the specific pattern of the last one. It's a trip wire laid exactly where the last intruder walked. It does nothing for the intruder who takes a different path, and there is always a different path, because capital and risk are extremely good at finding the route with no trip wire on it.

Real prevention would mean anticipating failure modes nobody has seen yet. That's not what backward-looking rulemaking can do, structurally. You cannot write a rule against a mechanism you haven't observed. So the system settles for the next best thing: detecting recurrences of known failures, and calling that safety.

This distinction matters because it changes what we should expect from regulation. A detection system catches the same crime twice. It has nothing to say about the crime that hasn't been invented yet.

The Cost Falls on Everyone, Not Just the Risk-Takers

The part of this story that gets missed most often: the burden of these backward-looking rules doesn't land on the reckless actors who caused the original crisis. It lands on everyone who comes after them, most of whom were never going to cause a crisis in the first place.

Dodd-Frank was written to rein in the largest, most systemically dangerous banks. By 2018, its compliance costs had exceeded $38.9 billion and 82.9 million hours of paperwork. The institutions that absorbed that burden most heavily were not the megabanks it targeted. They were community banks, the smallest, least risky players in the system, who lacked the scale to spread that cost across a large balance sheet. Between 2012 and 2019, the number of community banks in the US fell by 30 percent. A rule aimed at Too Big To Fail ended up thinning out the small and careful.

That is not an edge case. It is the default outcome of retrospective regulation. A $2 million compliance cost is a rounding error for a billion-dollar institution and an existential threat to a $10 million one. The rule doesn't discriminate between the bad actor and the careful newcomer. It just charges both of them the same toll, and the careful newcomer is usually the one who can least afford it.

So the pattern compounds. The next crisis is missed because the rule was aimed at the last one. And in the meantime, the rule quietly makes it harder for smaller, newer, more cautious participants to survive at all, while the largest players, best equipped to absorb compliance cost, simply build it into the cost of doing business.

The Pattern Beyond Finance

Once you see this shape, it stops looking like a finance story.

Airport security since 2001 has been built almost entirely around refighting the last attack. Shoes come off because of one man in 2001. Liquids get bagged because of a foiled plot in 2006. Each new rule is a monument to a specific, already-thwarted plan. None of it anticipates the next method, because the next method is, again, whatever hasn't been regulated yet. Security researchers have a name for this: fighting the last war. It produces enormous inconvenience for millions of ordinary travelers and very little evidence of stopping anyone using a new approach.

Pandemic preparedness follows the same arc. Health systems rebuilt their stockpiles and protocols after SARS, then again after H1N1, each time optimized for a pathogen with a specific transmission profile. COVID-19 exposed gaps that nobody had built defenses against, because those gaps hadn't mattered in the last outbreak.

Cybersecurity budgets get allocated the same way. Firewalls and detection systems get hardened against last year's breach vector, while the next attacker simply routes around the exact defense that was built to stop the previous one.

Wrong Question, Wrong Target

None of this is an argument against rules. Detection has real value, catching repeat offenders and known failure modes is worth doing. The mistake is calling it prevention and expecting it to do prevention's job.

But there's a deeper mistake underneath that one. Every cycle of post-crisis rulemaking asks the same question: what new rule would have stopped this? It rarely asks the more useful question: who should never have been allowed into the system in the first place?

Those are different problems, and institutions keep solving the wrong one. A new rule polices behavior after the fact, for everyone, forever. It adds a checkpoint to every transaction, every institution, every participant, on the theory that if the rule is tight enough, the bad actor will be caught somewhere in the friction. But the bad actor is one participant among thousands. Everyone else, the overwhelming majority who were never going to cause the next crisis, absorbs the cost of a rule written for someone else's misconduct.

Keeping bad actors out of the system in the first place is a fundamentally different posture. It's not about writing a more detailed rulebook for a hundred thousand participants. It's about better identification, screening, and accountability for the handful who actually pose the risk. Community banks didn't need to lose 30 percent of their ranks to prevent the next AIG. AIG needed better oversight of who was allowed to concentrate that much risk in the first place. The rule punished the population. The problem was a participant.

This is why backward-looking rulemaking keeps failing in the same shape. It treats the system as guilty until proven innocent and asks everyone to prove it, repeatedly, at growing cost. It rarely asks the sharper question of who shouldn't have had access to the system at all. A system that filters entry is a fundamentally different design than a system that polices everyone's exit.

The honest position is this: a rule built after a crisis will reliably stop that crisis from repeating in exactly that form, and will reliably miss whatever comes next, while quietly taxing everyone who wasn't the problem. Keeping the wrong people and the wrong institutions out of the system in the first place doesn't have that flaw. It doesn't need to predict the next crisis's shape. It just needs to keep asking who belongs here, continuously, before the damage is done rather than after.
