The Algorithm Didn't Radicalize You. Engagement Did.

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Aug 1, 2026
7 min read
featured image

Blaming "the algorithm" is the most comfortable explanation in modern life, because it lets everyone skip the harder question. It wasn't a recommendation engine that decided outrage deserves more attention than nuance. We did, collectively, one click at a time.

I don't say that to defend platform design. I say it because the popular fix, better algorithms, keeps failing for a reason nobody wants to sit with.

The consensus everyone repeats

The standard story goes like this: recommendation systems optimize for engagement, engagement rewards extremity, and extremity radicalizes people who never asked to be radicalized. The fix, in this telling, is regulatory pressure on platforms to redesign their ranking systems, add friction, or open up their black-box models to audit.

Every part of that story is true as far as it goes. It just stops one step short of the actual mechanism.

The turn

A recommendation algorithm isn't handed a worldview. It's trained on behavior, on what people actually click, watch, and linger over, not what they claim to want when a researcher asks them in a survey. It surfaces extremity because extremity performs better than nuance, and it performs better because a huge number of individually reasonable people reward it with their attention, every single day, without noticing they're voting.

The algorithm isn't a bug in the code. It's a mirror with a feedback loop attached.

That's what makes "fix the algorithm" such an unsatisfying answer. Any system that ranks content by predicted engagement, run by anyone, trained on anyone's data, will drift toward the same place, because the underlying incentive hasn't changed: attention converts to revenue, and outrage reliably captures attention. Swap the engineering team, change the platform, rewrite the ranking model from scratch, and the pull toward extremity reappears within months, because it was never really coming from the model. It was coming from what the model was told to optimize for.

What actually shifts the outcome

The lever that moves the needle isn't a smarter algorithm. It's a different business model. Platforms funded by subscriptions rather than ad-driven engagement have far less structural reason to chase outrage, because their revenue doesn't depend on how long you doomscroll. That's a commercial fact, not a moral one, and it's a far more reliable predictor of a platform's behavior than any ethics statement it publishes.

So evaluate the platforms you use by how they make money, not by how thoughtfully they talk about AI safety. And notice your own feed the way you'd audit a financial statement: what you keep engaging with is what you'll keep being fed. That's not a defect in the system. It's the system working exactly as intended, on exactly the inputs you gave it.
