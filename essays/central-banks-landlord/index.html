Central Banks Can't Rescue You From Your Landlord

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Jul 22, 2026
9 min read
featured image

Every time a central bank cuts rates, the headlines promise relief. Cheaper mortgages, cheaper borrowing, a lighter load on household budgets. And every time, rent barely moves. Renters wait for the relief that was promised on the news and it never quite arrives at their door.

That's not a policy failure. It's a mismatch between the problem and the tool being used to solve it.

The consensus everyone repeats

The intuitive story is that cheaper borrowing eventually filters down to cheaper housing costs across the board: developers build more because financing is cheaper, landlords pass on lower mortgage costs, and buyers face less competition because credit is more accessible to everyone at once. Lower rates, in this telling, are a tide that lifts affordability generally.

It's intuitive. It's also solving for the wrong variable.

The turn

Monetary policy changes the cost of buying an asset. It does almost nothing to the cost of using one when the supply of that asset is fixed in the short run, which describes housing in almost every desirable city on earth. Rents are set by local supply and demand: how many units exist, how fast new ones get approved and built, and how many people want to live within commuting distance of the same jobs. None of that responds meaningfully to an interest rate decision made in a capital hundreds of miles away.

A rate cut makes it cheaper to buy the building. It doesn't make there be more buildings.

Worse, cheaper credit often works against renters directly. When borrowing gets cheaper, investors and buyers with existing capital compete harder for the same limited housing stock, bidding up purchase prices. That pushes homeownership further out of reach for first-time buyers and, in many markets, increases the pool of people who have no choice but to keep renting, adding demand pressure to an already tight rental market. The policy engineered to help affordability can tighten it instead, for the people who needed help the most.

Where the actual lever is

The fix for expensive rent is boring and local: zoning that allows more units to be built, faster permitting, less procedural friction between "we need more housing" and a crane on site. None of that is a central bank's job, and none of it moves on the timeline of a rate decision. It moves on the timeline of city council meetings, planning boards, and years of unglamorous permitting reform.

So stop waiting for a rate cut to fix your rent. That's not the fight it belongs to. If you want your housing costs to actually change, the leverage is local: zoning hearings, permitting rules, supply-side politics that never make the front page. Direct your attention there, and you're arguing with the part of the system that can actually move the number.
