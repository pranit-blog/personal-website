The Compliance Department Exists to Be Blamed

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Aug 8, 2026
8 min read
featured image

Every bank has a compliance department. Almost none of them have the power to stop a bad decision. They can flag it, memo it, escalate it, and lose. What they cannot do, in most institutions I've supervised or worked inside, is say no and have it stick.

That single design flaw explains more about financial scandals than any regulation written after the fact.

I spent years on the other side of the table, reviewing how banks and brokers were actually run rather than how their org charts said they were run. The pattern repeats with almost boring regularity. A scandal breaks. Journalists ask how it happened. The institution points to its compliance function, its policies, its training modules. Then, quietly, someone in compliance gets moved out, and the executives who overruled them keep their titles.

The department wasn't absent. It usually saw the problem coming. It just had no authority to stop it, and full accountability once it went wrong.

The consensus everyone repeats

The standard fix, the one boards reach for after every failure, is to hire more compliance staff and expand the department's budget. It feels responsible. It shows up well in a shareholder letter. And it treats the problem as a capacity issue: not enough people watching, not enough training, not enough tools.

If capacity were the issue, the biggest, best-funded institutions would fail the least. They don't. Some of the largest compliance functions I've reviewed sat inside some of the most consequential failures, fully staffed, fully aware, fully ignored.

A department with responsibility but no authority isn't a control. It's a liability shield with a badge.

The turn

Compliance in most institutions can advise, escalate, and document. It cannot veto a revenue-generating decision on its own. That decision sits with business leadership, who are measured, paid, and promoted on numbers compliance has no stake in. When compliance raises a concern and the business overrides it, the paper trail that survives is compliance's own memo, warning of exactly what later went wrong. It reads, after the fact, like proof the control failed. It was actually proof the control worked and got ignored.

This isn't cynicism about individuals. Most compliance officers I worked alongside took the job seriously and lost sleep over calls they couldn't win. The structure is the problem, not the people in it. And the structure creates a strange incentive for leadership: knowing compliance will absorb the blame if something goes wrong makes it marginally easier to take the risk in the first place. The backstop was supposed to prevent recklessness. Instead it subsidizes it.

What to look for instead

If you're evaluating a financial institution, whether as a regulator, an investor, or someone deciding where to work, the useful question isn't "how big is compliance." It's "can the head of compliance kill a deal alone, without needing the CEO's permission to say no." Almost nowhere can they. The rare places where they can are usually the ones that don't end up explaining themselves to a regulator two years later.

Stop grading institutions on how thorough their compliance department looks on paper. Grade them on how much power that department actually has to lose an argument on purpose, and win anyway.
