AI Doesn't Run on GPUs. It Runs on Cheap Capital.

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Sep 21, 2026
7 min read
featured image

The loudest conversation about AI right now is about fear. Fear that it takes your job. Fear that it writes its own successor. Fear that nobody can slow it down. Last week Dario Amodei published an essay titled We Must Pace the Frontier. Within hours Sam Altman and Elon Musk had endorsed it. Congress is agitated. Employees are resigning with warnings.

I think the fear is aimed at the wrong thing. AI does not need a regulator, a treaty or a brave essay to slow it down. It may slow itself down for a much older reason. The money is getting expensive.

Every AI company will tell you its scarcest input is compute. Nvidia chips, power, data centres. That is what the earnings calls discuss. It is the wrong answer. The scarcest input in AI is capital that is cheap enough to borrow in the hundreds of billions before a single dollar of return shows up. And the thing most likely to make that capital expensive is not sitting in Silicon Valley. It is sitting in a strait 21 miles wide between Iran and Oman.

What a supervisor looks at first

When I worked in banking supervision, the first thing we examined was never the loan book. It was funding. A bank with excellent assets and unstable funding is still a bank that can fail. Lehman Brothers did not collapse because its mortgages went bad overnight. It collapsed because the people lending it money overnight stopped answering the phone.

The AI industry now has the funding profile of a bank in a boom. Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon and Meta plan to spend roughly $725 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, up 77% from about $410 billion in 2025. Goldman Sachs expects $5.3 trillion between 2025 and 2030 from those four alone.

Until 18 months ago they paid for this out of cash flow. Not anymore. FactSet estimates the debt share of hyperscaler capex rose from 9% in 2024 to 32% by mid-2026. Alphabet raised $84.75 billion in equity in June. Oracle is raising $40 billion in debt and equity, and S&P downgraded it to BBB-, one notch above junk, because of what it has promised to build for OpenAI. Amazon's free cash flow is projected to turn negative this year.

Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan put the total new debt the tech sector needs at $1.5 trillion over the next few years. That is not a capex story. That is a leverage story.

Why everyone assumes the brakes don't exist

The consensus, shared by the people cheering AI and the people terrified of it, is that nothing can stop this. Demand for capacity is absorbed as fast as it is built. Balance sheets are strong. Net leverage sits well below downgrade thresholds for four of the five hyperscalers. The S&P 500 closed at 7,637 on September 17, about 2% off its record. If you believe AI is unstoppable, the numbers seem to agree with you.

The consensus is right about one thing. The assets are real. Nobody is building empty warehouses. The bet on AI demand may well pay off.

But a leveraged bet on a good asset can still lose. It loses when the cost of carrying it changes faster than the asset can pay you back. That is the brake nobody is looking at. Which brings us to oil.

The chain nobody is pricing

Here is the chain, link by link.

Iran has kept the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed since March. One fifth of the world's oil normally passes through it. The International Energy Agency calls this the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market. Brent peaked at $114 in May and is back above $100 after Houthi drones forced Saudi Arabia to shut its East-West pipeline, the one route that avoids the strait.

Oil moves inflation. US consumer prices rose 4.2% in May, the highest in three years. Petrol is $4.31 a gallon. August CPI jumped again on energy.

Inflation moves the Fed. On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised rates to 3.75% to 4.00%. It was the first hike in more than three years, delivered by a new chairman over the loud objections of the president. The 10-year Treasury yields 4.94%.

Rates move the cost of capital. And the cost of capital is the one line item that hits every part of the AI stack at once. It raises the coupon on the next $30 billion bond. It raises the discount rate on earnings that will not arrive until 2030. It makes an equity raise at a $965 billion valuation harder to justify.

A 2019-era hyperscaler funding 91% of capex from cash barely noticed interest rates. A 2026-era hyperscaler funding a third of it from bonds notices every 25 basis points. The industry rewired its own sensitivity to oil, and it did so at the exact moment oil became volatile.

We have a preview. When Meta announced a $30 billion bond in October 2025, its stock fell more than 20% in a week. Nothing had changed about Meta's AI. What changed was the price of financing it. Now imagine that repricing across four companies, $725 billion, and a rate cycle that has turned.

The second brake: petrodollars

There is a second link between oil and AI, and it is more direct.

Gulf sovereign wealth funds hold somewhere between $4 trillion and $6 trillion. They invested about $119 billion in 2025, most of it in the United States. Abu Dhabi's MGX closed a $49 billion AI fund in July. It co-led OpenAI's $122 billion raise in March and joined Anthropic's $65 billion round in May. Anthropic's own CEO wrote to staff that the Gulf holds "easily $100B or more" and that without it, staying at the frontier becomes substantially harder.

That is concentration risk, and a regulator would have flagged it years ago. The frontier labs, the layer of the AI economy with the least cash flow and the greatest need for patient capital, have made a single region their largest external source of it.

That region is now under fiscal strain. Saudi Arabia posted a $33.5 billion deficit in the first quarter, its largest in nearly eight years, because production fell from about 10 million barrels a day to 6.9 million. Qatar, Kuwait and Iraq have almost no way around Hormuz at all. The Saudi Public Investment Fund has cut its international allocation from 30% to 20% of its portfolio. It has walked away from LIV Golf and withdrawn a $200 million gift to the Met Opera.

The money is not disappearing. MGX proves that. But it is being pulled home, and the Council on Foreign Relations makes the uncomfortable point that there is no equally large pool waiting to replace it. Not Norway, not Singapore, not Japan.

So the same oil shock that raises the price of debt for hyperscalers also tightens the equity tap for frontier labs. Two channels, one cause.

The slowdown that arrives without anyone deciding it

This is why I am less scared than most people. Not because the technology is harmless, but because the fear assumes an industry with unlimited fuel. The industry does not have that. It has a funding model that was built in a world of cheap money and Gulf surpluses, and both of those conditions are now in question.

Nobody has to vote for a slowdown. No lab has to break ranks. A few more quarters of $100 oil, a Fed that keeps hiking, a bond that prices badly, a sovereign fund that passes on a round. That is how a capex boom decelerates. Quietly, through the cost of capital, while everyone is still arguing about whether it can be stopped.

Read the pacing essay through that lens. Amodei's stated reasons are safety and alignment, and I have no evidence they are not sincere. But notice the timing. The essay landed four days before the Fed's first hike in three years, in the middle of a $1.5 trillion debt cycle, from a company whose own CEO has said the frontier gets substantially harder without Gulf money, and from an industry in which both leading labs are widely expected to seek public listings. If you are a lab that suspects it will not be able to scale at the old pace, a public commitment to slow down is a remarkably useful thing to have on record. It makes you sound responsible about risk. It resets investor expectations about capex and model cadence before a listing, rather than after. And it means that when the next round comes in smaller, or the next data centre is delayed, nobody calls it a setback. They call it discipline. Altman and Musk endorsing it within hours does not weaken that reading. Everyone in the race benefits from a slower race if the money is about to get expensive.

The last great capex boom in American history, the railroads, did not end because the country stopped needing trains. It ended because the money got expensive.

What this means for you

If you hold AI stocks, or an index that is mostly AI stocks, you are not just long chips and demand. You are short interest rates and long Gulf stability, whether you signed up for that or not. The Shiller price-to-earnings ratio hit 41.6 in May, the second highest reading in 140 years. Valuations that high are a statement about the discount rate as much as about the earnings.

The practical reframe is simple. Stop reading AI news for the compute number. Start reading it for the funding number. How much of the next capex figure is borrowed. What the bond priced at. Who anchored the round.

AI will not run out of GPUs. What it can run out of is cheap capital. And right now the price of that capital is being set in Hormuz, not in San Francisco.
