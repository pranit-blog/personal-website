Your Startup Isn't Disrupting Anything. It's Just Skipping the Paperwork.

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Jul 15, 2026
6 min read
featured image

A lot of what gets called "disruption" in fintech is regulatory arbitrage wearing a hoodie. Not all of it. Some startups genuinely build something faster, cheaper, and better than what existed before. But a large share of the "10x better" story quietly depends on one fact nobody puts in the pitch deck: the incumbent has to hold capital and compliance costs that the startup, for now, doesn't.

I spent years watching this pattern from the regulator's chair, and it repeats with remarkable consistency.

The consensus everyone repeats

The startup narrative is familiar: incumbents are slow, bureaucratic, and customer-hostile because legacy systems and legacy thinking hold them back. Startups move fast, obsess over the customer, and build products incumbents are too clumsy to ship. Speed and focus explain the gap.

Speed and focus are real advantages. They're rarely the whole story, and almost never the biggest part of it.

The turn

A licensed bank has to hold capital against risk, maintain compliance infrastructure, and absorb the cost of examinations, reporting, and audits that never show up on a product roadmap. A new entrant offering a similar service before it holds the same license, or while operating through a lighter-touch category, doesn't carry those costs yet. That gap isn't innovation. It's a temporary pricing advantage created by a regulatory perimeter that hasn't caught up.

Most "10x better" in fintech is really "10x less regulated, for now."

The pattern usually resolves one of two ways. Either regulators extend the same requirements to the new category once it reaches meaningful scale, and the startup's cost structure suddenly looks a lot more like the incumbent's, or the startup never reaches the scale where anyone bothers to regulate it and stays a niche product. What almost never happens is a permanent, structural cost advantage that survives contact with the same rulebook everyone else operates under.

The question that actually tells you something

When you're evaluating a fast-growing fintech startup, whether as an investor, an employee, or a customer, don't ask what makes their product better. Ask what happens to their unit economics on the day they're required to hold the same capital and compliance costs as the bank they're replacing. If the answer is "the business still works," you're probably looking at real innovation. If the answer is a shrug, or a vague promise about scale efficiencies, you're looking at arbitrage with a growth chart attached.

That question won't make headlines the way "disruption" does. It will save you from mistaking a regulatory gap for a business model.
