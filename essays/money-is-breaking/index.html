Money Is Breaking in Slow Motion

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Aug 13, 2026
6 min read
featured image

We are living through something subtle. Not a crash, not a collapse. A slow, grinding reassessment of what money is actually for, and whether any single instrument can still do the job.

In 2025 and into early 2026, stress in financial markets built quietly but meaningfully. U.S. Treasuries, long regarded as the world's safest asset, saw significant volatility as fiscal deficits expanded and real yields shifted. Investors began reconsidering what it means for sovereign debt to be a safe haven. This sentiment crystallised in what markets called the "Sell America" trade: a broad rotation away from dollar-denominated assets.

At the same time, gold surged to record and near-record levels. Not because of speculation. Because investors wanted out of paper promises and into something with a track record measured in millennia.

Bitcoin told a more complicated story. After peaking above previous cycle highs in 2025, it gave back much of those gains as macro volatility returned and liquidity tightened. The crypto faithful had promised a new store of value. Markets delivered a reminder that narrative and function are not the same thing.

These three moves, Treasury weakness, gold's ascent, Bitcoin's retreat, are not random. They reflect a single, deeper tension: money is being asked to do three things at once, and it keeps failing at all of them.

The structural flaw

Money has always been expected to serve three distinct functions: store value reliably over time, enable everyday exchange and transactions, and provide a stable unit of account, a consistent pricing benchmark.

The problem is not new. But it has become newly visible.

In Argentina, persistent inflation has driven people to hold U.S. dollars while transacting in pesos, a forced separation of saving from spending. In Turkey, gold has served as the household savings vehicle while the lira continued losing ground. In Venezuela, stablecoins quietly emerged as a parallel pricing layer for major purchases, even as small everyday transactions still flowed through local cash.

In each case, ordinary people solved an institutional failure by routing different monetary functions through different instruments. They did this informally, imperfectly, and often at significant cost.

No single asset in 2025-2026 consistently fulfilled all three monetary roles: safety, liquidity, and stable pricing. People are already routing these functions through different instruments, informally, by necessity.

The events of 2025-2026 suggest this dynamic is no longer confined to emerging markets. When U.S. bond markets wobble, when gold outperforms Treasuries as a safe haven, when the world's reserve currency faces credibility questions, the informal layering behaviour scales up globally.

Informal layering is already happening

When trust in a currency erodes, people adapt. They earn and transact in whatever the local system requires. They save in something more reliable: dollars, gold, stablecoins. They price major obligations in whichever unit feels most stable.

The question is not whether people will separate money's functions. They already are. The question is whether we could design that separation intentionally.

This monetary layering happens bottom-up, without design, driven by necessity. The system works, imperfectly, because human beings are relentlessly pragmatic about protecting their economic lives. But informal layering comes with costs: friction, opacity, regulatory risk, and the constant cognitive overhead of managing multiple instruments with different risk profiles.

A proposal: purpose-built money

Rather than searching for one superior asset to replace fiat, or patching a fundamentally overloaded system, consider a purpose-built financial stack with distinct instruments, each optimised for a single monetary function.

This is not a description of something that exists. It is a proposal shaped by the realities of 2025-2026 macro stress. Three tokens, three jobs, one coherent system.

DA, a stable unit of account. A reference denomination for pricing and contracts. DA stays consistent against purchasing power over time, not spent, not saved, but used to denominate. Prices, wages, and obligations are set in DA, providing a stable cognitive anchor even if underlying currencies fluctuate.

DS, a spend token. Optimised for exchange. DS is everyday money: wages paid, goods purchased, transfers made. Crucially, DS would carry a small holding cost, demurrage, to discourage hoarding and encourage circulation. Money designed to move, not sit.

DV, a save token. Optimised for wealth preservation. DV is not directly spendable, holders convert it to DS when they want to transact. Backed by diversified, low-volatility assets: not just Treasuries, but hard assets including commodities and precious metals.

Historical echoes

These ideas are not without precedent.

In the early 1990s, Brazil deployed the URV, Unidade Real de Valor, as a transitional unit of account, anchoring prices psychologically even as inflation continued to erode the existing currency. By separating the pricing function from the payment function, policymakers gave people a stable cognitive anchor, which was enough to break the inflationary spiral.

In 1930s Austria, the town of Wörgl introduced a local currency with demurrage, a holding cost that made it expensive to hoard. Economic activity accelerated visibly as money circulated faster. The experiment was shut down by central bank authorities not because it failed, but because it succeeded too conspicuously.

What 2025-2026 adds to these historical cases is a digital infrastructure capable of making such rules programmable, transparent, and enforceable without political discretion.

Why 2025-2026 makes this urgent

Three signals stand out.

Treasury weakness. The assumption that U.S. sovereign debt is a perpetual safe haven cracked. Fiscal imbalances and shifting global sentiment produced bond sell-offs that reminded markets: sovereign debt is still debt.

Gold's resurgence. Gold is not a relic. Its surge in 2025-2026 reflects renewed global demand for assets whose value does not depend on any government's promise. That is a meaningful signal about what people actually trust.

Bitcoin's pullback. After impressive rallies, crypto reversed sharply as macro stress returned. Volatility disqualifies an asset from reliably serving any of money's three core functions.

Together, these signals point to the same conclusion: the problem is architectural. Money has been asked to do too many things with a single instrument, and the strain is showing.

The deeper shift

The instability of 2025-2026 is not a collapse. It is a reminder.

When sovereign bonds wobble, people rotate into hard assets. When inflation persists, people find stores of value outside local money. When one system fails, human beings route around it. They always have.

Gold, Bitcoin, stablecoins, and foreign dollars are all part of this story. But without structural separation of monetary roles, these workarounds remain ad hoc: costly, imperfect, and inaccessible to those without the financial sophistication or capital to deploy them.

A purpose-built three-layer monetary system would formalise what the most financially resourceful people already do instinctively, and make it available, by design, to everyone.

The question is not whether money's functions will eventually be separated. The question is whether that separation happens by design or by accident, with structural rules or with improvised chaos.

We have a rare window to think clearly about this. It would be worth using it.
