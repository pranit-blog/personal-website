Your Brain Works Like ChatGPT. That Should Unsettle You.

PS
Pranit Saindani
Former banking regulator, RBI · BNP Paribas · Nasdaq
Aug 17, 2026
7 min read
featured image

You probably already know that AI LLMs like ChatGPT work by predicting the next word. That's it. Feed it a sequence of tokens, and it calculates the most probable continuation. No understanding, no experience, no consciousness. Just an extraordinarily sophisticated pattern-completion engine, trained on the accumulated text of human civilisation. This simple ability to predict text is what allows these models to write code, pass bar exams, and hold conversations that feel disturbingly human.

So what if your brain is doing something remarkably similar? Neuroscience currently assumes that human intelligence is fundamentally different from that of machines. But what if it isn't? What if human intelligence works the same way: analysing existing data and predicting the next likely outcome?

Think about the last time you reached for your phone before a notification arrived. Or finished a colleague's sentence before they did. Or walked into a room and felt something was wrong before you could explain why. These feel like intuition. They're not. They're predictions, and your brain is running them constantly, on everything, without asking your permission.

It turns out, there's serious science behind that. And the answer is more uncomfortable and more useful than most people expect.

The standard story is wrong

For most of the 20th century, neuroscience operated on a simple model: sensory data arrives, the brain processes it, behaviour emerges. Input, processing, output. Clean, linear, intuitive. It's also wrong.

The framework that has replaced it is called predictive coding, or predictive processing. The core claim: your brain is not waiting for information and then reacting. It is constantly generating predictions about what it expects to experience next. Incoming sensory data doesn't build your reality. It merely checks and corrects the model your brain already holds.

You are not experiencing the world directly. You are experiencing your brain's best guess, updated when that guess turns out to be wrong.

The brain runs the same loop at every level: predict, compare, update. When predictions land, the system runs efficiently on minimal signal. When they miss, the brain fires a prediction error, a correction signal that forces the model to revise. This happens thousands of times per second, across every sense, without you noticing.

Karl Friston, with over 400,000 academic citations, arguably the most cited neuroscientist alive, has formalised this into the Free Energy Principle. The proposal is sweeping. Minimising prediction error is the single unifying objective of all brain function. Not just perception. Not just language. Everything: attention, emotion, motor control, decision-making. All of it reduces to one thing. Build better predictions, correct worse ones.

Sound familiar?

The parallel is closer than it's comfortable to admit

LLMs are trained by minimising prediction error. The model predicts the next token, compares it against the actual one, calculates the error, adjusts its weights. Repeat across hundreds of billions of examples, and something that looks disturbingly like intelligence emerges.

Your brain was shaped by the same logic. Not on a static dataset, but across a lifetime. Every misjudged social interaction, every unexpected outcome, every sentence that ended differently than you expected, your neural connections adjusted. Your model got refined. The mechanism is biological, the substrate is entirely different, but the objective function is the same. Predict accurately, update when wrong.

This is not coincidence. Early AI researchers drew heavily from neuroscience. Attention mechanisms, hierarchical processing, error-driven learning: all have roots in theories of how the brain works. The resemblance between LLMs and predictive processing is, in part, by design.

The evidence keeps tightening. A 2024 paper in Nature by researchers at Massachusetts General Hospital identified individual neurons in the human prefrontal cortex encoding the precise phonetic structure of words, consonants, vowels, syllables, before those words are spoken. The brain isn't figuring out the sounds after it decides to speak. It pre-loads the full prediction first. That is a biological system running something that looks very much like next-token prediction, at the level of individual neurons.

We built machines that predict text, and they started doing things we thought only minds could do. Now neuroscience tells us minds work by predicting too. That's either a remarkable coincidence, or it points at something fundamental about what intelligence actually is.

But you are not just a very complicated autocomplete

Before you spiral into an existential crisis, here is where the parallel breaks down. And it breaks down importantly.

LLMs predict tokens. That's the entirety of their domain. Your brain predicts across every sensory channel simultaneously: sight, sound, touch, the position of your limbs, the state of your organs, the emotional temperature of a room. When you reach for a coffee mug without looking, your brain has already predicted its weight and position. When you walk into a meeting and sense something is off before anyone speaks, you've modelled the dynamics from micro-signals in posture and expression that never even reached your conscious awareness.

The difference runs deeper than breadth. Neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that emotions themselves are predictions. Not reactions to events, but your brain pre-loading the bodily state it anticipates you'll need. The anxiety before a difficult conversation doesn't fire because of the conversation. It fires before it. Your brain is already preparing for what it predicts is coming.

No LLM has a body to regulate or anything genuinely at stake. Its predictions are computationally costly but existentially free. Every prediction your brain makes is backed by real consequences: social, physical, emotional.

That's not a small difference. Consider expertise. A chess grandmaster doesn't consciously analyse lines. Their brain has built, through thousands of hours of play, predictive models so refined that the right move surfaces before deliberate reasoning begins. That effortlessness isn't the absence of cognition. It's prediction error dropping to near zero. LLMs can reproduce the output of expertise. They cannot build it the way humans do: through embodied experience, real failure, and correction over years of skin-in-the-game learning.

So are LLMs basically minds?

When confronted with this parallel, most people reach for one of two exits. Either LLMs must be basically conscious, they predict like we do, so they must think like we do. Or the comparison is shallow and should be dismissed.

Both exits are wrong.

Prediction is a necessary condition for intelligence. It is not a sufficient one. LLMs show that prediction at scale produces something that genuinely resembles understanding, fluency, and reasoning. But the human brain built a similar architecture through a radically different route: embodied, emotionally regulated, continuous across a lifetime, shaped by consequences that actually mattered.

What LLMs have really done, beyond the obvious applications, is force a confrontation with a question we were happy to leave vague. What is intelligence, actually? The uncomfortable answer is that language, pattern recognition, and analogical reasoning may all be emergent properties of prediction done at sufficient scale. That's unsettling. It's also probably true.

Consciousness remains completely unexplained. So does genuine curiosity: the drive to seek out situations where you will be wrong, because you want to learn. So does the capacity to act against your own predictions, to choose the improbable path, to create something your model has no basis for anticipating. These are not small things. They are, arguably, the things that matter most.

What you do with this

Here is where this stops being philosophy and starts being useful.

If your brain is a prediction engine, intelligence is not a fixed endowment. Not some tank of fluid capacity you either have or don't. It is a model. And models can be built, refined, and expanded. You have genuine agency over the quality of your predictions.

Curiosity is not a personality trait. It is a cognitive strategy: deliberately seeking situations where your current model will be wrong. The most intellectually alive people you know aren't curious because they were born that way. They keep choosing to be surprised. They enter rooms where their predictions will fail, and stay long enough to update.

Learning feels uncomfortable for a precise reason. It requires your model to revise. The friction isn't a signal that you're not smart enough. It's a signal that learning is actually happening. Discomfort is the mechanism, not an obstacle to it.

And the rise of LLMs doesn't make human intelligence redundant. It clarifies what human intelligence actually is: not the retrieval and recombination of patterns, but the capacity to build a model of reality from inside a living, motivated, embodied existence, and to update it honestly when the world doesn't match your expectations.

That last part, updating honestly, turns out to be the hard part. For LLMs and for humans alike.

ChatGPT predicts the next word. You predict the next moment of your life, shaped by everything you've experienced, everything you've gotten wrong, and every time you stayed curious enough to update. The machines are catching up on the prediction part. The rest is still yours.
